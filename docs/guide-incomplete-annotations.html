Incomplete annotations and non-model organisms

Why bramble helps most when the annotation is missing transcripts.

The problem with aligning to a transcriptome

No annotation contains every transcript a sample expresses. Human annotations still disagree with each other, a sizable part of cellular RNA is transcriptional noise, and annotations of most other organisms are largely automated. When reads are aligned straight to the annotated transcripts, a read from a missing transcript has nowhere to go but the most similar annotated one, so it is counted toward the wrong isoform.

What bramble does differently

Aligned to the genome, a read from a missing transcript lands where it came from. bramble only projects it onto annotated transcripts it actually fits, exon by exon; if it fits none, it isn't projected, instead of being forced onto a wrong one.

The bramble paper measured this by removing transcripts from the annotation at random. The fewer transcripts kept, the larger bramble's advantage: with 20% of transcripts kept, for simulated PacBio reads, minimap2 + bramble + oarfish reached a Pearson correlation of 0.62 with the true counts, against 0.45 for minimap2 + oarfish on the transcriptome, with a fifth of the mean absolute error. The gains are largest for long reads, which span more splice junctions, but short reads benefit too.

Practical advice

  • Use the most complete annotation available. For human, a comprehensive annotation such as GENCODE or CHESS 3 rather than a reduced one. For other organisms, Ensembl or RefSeq annotations where they exist.
  • Use the annotation that matches the genome build the reads were aligned to, with the same chromosome names.
  • Consider extending the annotation with transcripts assembled from your own data; see Sample-specific annotations with StringTie.
  • Reads that don't project aren't lost. They are still in the genome BAM, where you can look at them, for example to find unannotated genes or isoforms.
